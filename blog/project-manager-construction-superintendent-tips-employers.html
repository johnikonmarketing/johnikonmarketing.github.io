Project Manager / Construction Superintendent Tips for Employers

Hire for Field Leadership, Not Just a Strong Resume

Hiring a Project Manager or Construction Superintendent can have a direct effect on schedule reliability, trade coordination, safety, quality, and client confidence. For employers, the challenge is that job titles do not always tell the full story. A candidate may have managed budgets effectively but have limited jobsite leadership experience. Another may be an excellent field superintendent who needs support with reporting, procurement, or owner communication.

The best hiring process begins by defining what the position must own. On a commercial tenant build-out, the priority may be coordinating multiple trades in a tight sequence while maintaining code compliance. On a residential project, the role may require more frequent homeowner communication, finish-quality control, and quick decisions when site conditions change. Clarify whether the person will manage one large project, several smaller projects, subcontractors, self-performed work, or all of the above.

Employers should ask candidates for specific examples rather than accepting broad statements such as “I managed projects from start to finish.” Useful follow-up questions include:

  • Tell me about a schedule that began to slip. What did you change, and what was the outcome?
  • Describe a disagreement between trades that affected production. How did you resolve it?
  • How do you document field changes before they become cost disputes?
  • What information do you need before releasing work to a subcontractor?
  • How do you verify that completed work is ready for inspection or the next trade?

Specific answers reveal how a candidate thinks under pressure, communicates with crews, and protects the project when conditions are less than ideal.

Match Experience to the Type of Work You Perform

One of the most practical Project Manager / Construction Superintendent tips for employers is to look beyond years of experience and examine the relevance of that experience. A superintendent who has spent years on ground-up commercial projects may be highly qualified, but a fast-paced renovation contractor may need someone who is comfortable working around occupied spaces, existing systems, and short turnaround windows.

Technical familiarity matters as well. John Dillon’s background includes more than 20 years in commercial construction, with hands-on experience in fire protection, electrical systems, code compliance, and general contracting. That type of cross-trade knowledge can be especially valuable when a project involves life-safety systems, electrical coordination, ceiling closures, inspections, or conflicts between mechanical, electrical, plumbing, and fire protection work.

Employers do not need to hire a specialist for every role. They do need a leader who knows when to ask questions, bring in the right trade partner, and stop work before an issue becomes concealed, unsafe, or expensive to correct.

Use a Practical Interview Process

Construction leadership is a practical discipline, so the interview process should include practical evaluation. Consider giving candidates a simple project scenario: an inspection fails, a critical material delivery is delayed, and the drywall contractor is scheduled to begin in two days. Ask the candidate to explain their first steps.

A capable Project Manager or Superintendent should discuss verifying the facts, assessing schedule impact, communicating early with affected parties, documenting decisions, and developing a recovery plan. Listen for whether they blame others or take ownership of coordinating a solution.

It is also helpful to involve the people who will work with the new hire. A lead estimator, office manager, operations leader, or senior field employee may notice communication habits that are easy to miss in a one-on-one interview. The goal is not to create unnecessary layers of approval; it is to confirm that the candidate can work effectively within your company’s real operating structure.

Set Clear Authority and Accountability From Day One

Many construction management problems are not caused by a lack of effort. They happen because no one has clearly defined who can make a decision, approve a change, contact the owner, or authorize additional work. A new superintendent cannot lead a job effectively if every field decision requires multiple calls. At the same time, a project manager should not be left to make financial commitments without established limits.

During onboarding, provide a written outline that addresses:

  • Who owns the master schedule and weekly look-ahead schedule
  • Who approves change orders, subcontractor invoices, and purchase orders
  • Who communicates directly with owners, architects, inspectors, and vendors
  • How daily reports, progress photos, RFIs, and meeting notes are handled
  • What issues must be escalated immediately
  • What safety and quality documentation is required on every project

Clear expectations reduce duplicated effort and prevent important decisions from falling between office and field responsibilities.

Build Consistent Communication Habits

Employers should not assume that a strong superintendent or project manager will automatically understand how your company prefers to communicate. Establish a predictable rhythm. For example, require a short weekly project review that covers completed work, upcoming activities, material status, inspections, outstanding decisions, safety concerns, and financial risks.

Daily field reports should be useful, not burdensome. They can document manpower, weather conditions, deliveries, visitors, safety observations, progress, delays, and photos of work before it is covered. When a disagreement arises later, accurate records can protect the company and help resolve the issue without guesswork.

Communication should also move both directions. Field leaders need timely information from the office regarding contracts, revised drawings, permits, owner decisions, and procurement status. When the office withholds or delays that information, the jobsite is forced to react rather than plan.

Support Quality Control and Code Awareness

Quality control is not a final walk-through activity. It should happen before work starts, while it is underway, and before the next phase covers it. Employers can help by requiring pre-installation reviews for high-risk scopes such as fire protection, electrical rough-in, rated assemblies, waterproofing, and inspections.

For commercial and residential clients across the greater Nashville area, local conditions, permitting requirements, and existing building conditions can all affect execution. A manager or superintendent should be prepared to compare plans with field conditions, identify conflicts early, and coordinate corrections with the appropriate trade. Employers should provide access to current drawings, specifications, inspection requirements, and the right decision-makers.

Measure Performance Beyond Whether the Job Finished

Finishing a project is important, but it is not the only measure of leadership. Review performance based on schedule predictability, documentation quality, safety follow-through, punch-list management, subcontractor coordination, client communication, and the number of avoidable surprises that reached the owner or executive team.

Regular reviews give employers an opportunity to recognize good work and address gaps before they become habits. They also identify where the company’s systems need improvement. If several project leaders struggle with the same issue, the problem may be unclear procedures or inadequate resources rather than individual performance.

Choose Leaders Who Protect the Whole Project

The strongest construction leaders understand that their responsibility extends beyond their own task list. They protect the schedule, the budget, the workmanship, the safety of the crew, and the trust of the client. Employers who hire for practical judgment, define authority clearly, and provide consistent support are more likely to retain leaders who can manage difficult projects with confidence.

If you are reviewing how project leadership works on your commercial or residential jobs, consider where communication or field coordination breaks down most often. What is one responsibility you could clarify this week to help your project managers and superintendents lead more effectively?
