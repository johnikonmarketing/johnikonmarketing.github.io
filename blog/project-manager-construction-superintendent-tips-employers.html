Project Manager / Construction Superintendent Tips for Employers

Hire for the Actual Job, Not Just the Job Title

Employers often use “Project Manager” and “Construction Superintendent” interchangeably, but the strongest hiring decisions begin by defining what the position will truly own. A Project Manager is commonly responsible for budgets, contracts, procurement, client updates, change orders, subcontractor coordination, and the overall financial health of a project. A Construction Superintendent is usually the field leader who drives the daily schedule, manages site logistics, verifies workmanship, coordinates trades, and keeps safety visible on the jobsite.

On smaller commercial or residential projects, one experienced professional may handle both roles. That can work well when the scope, staffing, and reporting structure are realistic. However, asking one person to manage a complex budget, maintain owner communication, run daily field operations, and solve every trade conflict without support can create avoidable delays and burnout.

Before posting the role, write down the answers to these questions:

  • What types of projects will this person manage: commercial renovations, ground-up construction, tenant improvements, residential work, or specialty systems?
  • Will the role primarily work in the office, in the field, or across both?
  • Who has authority to approve purchases, changes, schedule adjustments, and subcontractor work?
  • What project-management software, reporting methods, and documentation standards are already in place?
  • What outcomes must improve within the first six months?

A clear job definition attracts candidates who understand the responsibility and helps employers compare applicants on the same criteria.

Look Beyond Years of Experience

Years in construction matter, but they do not automatically prove someone can lead a project. A candidate may have spent many years on a jobsite without managing schedules, budgets, subcontractor accountability, or owner expectations. Employers should look for evidence that a person has carried responsibility from planning through closeout.

Ask candidates to describe a project they managed in detail. Strong answers should explain the project scope, their specific authority, major risks, how they handled trade coordination, and what happened when conditions changed. Listen for ownership. “We handled it” is less useful than “I identified the issue, documented the impact, presented options, and coordinated the correction.”

For commercial work, practical familiarity with life-safety requirements, electrical coordination, inspections, and code-driven documentation can be especially valuable. John Dillon’s background includes fire protection, electrical systems, and general construction, which reflects an important hiring principle: construction leaders should understand how building systems affect one another. A superintendent who recognizes that a framing decision may interfere with electrical routing, fire protection coverage, access panels, or inspection requirements can prevent costly rework.

Use Scenario-Based Interviews

Traditional interview questions can reveal communication style, but scenario-based questions show how a candidate thinks under pressure. Present situations that mirror the work your company performs and ask the candidate to walk through their process.

Useful interview scenarios

  • A critical material delivery is delayed: Ask how the candidate would protect the schedule, communicate with the owner, and resequence work.
  • A subcontractor’s work does not meet the plans: Ask how they would document the issue, direct correction, and prevent the disagreement from becoming a schedule problem.
  • An inspector identifies a code concern: Ask what information they would gather before proposing a solution and who needs to be informed.
  • The owner requests a field change: Ask how the candidate would distinguish a simple adjustment from a change requiring pricing, approval, and schedule review.
  • Two trades need the same work area: Ask how they would set priorities, maintain safe access, and communicate the updated plan.

Pay attention to whether the candidate starts with documentation, communication, safety, and facts. A capable Project Manager or Construction Superintendent should not rely on verbal promises or make decisions without considering cost, schedule, quality, and contractual impact.

Evaluate Communication as a Core Construction Skill

Construction leadership depends on clear communication with owners, architects, engineers, inspectors, subcontractors, suppliers, and field crews. Employers should not treat communication as a soft skill that can be evaluated only by whether someone interviews well. Instead, ask for examples of project updates, meeting notes, daily reports, look-ahead schedules, or issue logs with confidential information removed.

Good construction communication is concise, timely, and specific. It identifies the issue, explains the impact, names the responsible party, and states the next step. For example, “Mechanical rough-in is incomplete in Area B, which delays ceiling framing. The mechanical contractor will provide a recovery plan by Thursday, and framing will shift to Area C until the sequence is confirmed.” That is far more useful than saying the project is “waiting on mechanical.”

Verify Field Credibility and Safety Leadership

Even a budget-focused Project Manager needs enough field understanding to recognize when a schedule, scope, or proposal does not match site conditions. Likewise, a superintendent must be able to lead crews without sacrificing documentation and financial discipline.

During the hiring process, ask how the candidate conducts site walks, reviews quality before work is covered, handles housekeeping, and addresses unsafe behavior. Look for leaders who understand that safety is not limited to a weekly meeting or paperwork requirement. It is built into planning, access, sequencing, material storage, equipment use, and daily expectations.

For roles involving electrical, fire protection, or other regulated building systems, confirm that the candidate knows when to involve qualified trade specialists. A strong leader does not pretend to know every technical answer; they recognize risk early and bring the right expertise into the conversation.

Check References with Purpose

Reference checks are most useful when they go beyond confirming employment dates. Ask former supervisors, clients, or trade partners how the candidate handled pressure, documentation, schedule recovery, conflict, and accountability. Questions such as “Would you trust this person to represent your company in a difficult owner meeting?” can provide more insight than a generic rating question.

Also ask about follow-through. Construction teams depend on leaders who close loops: they confirm decisions, track commitments, update stakeholders, and make sure corrections are completed. A candidate with technical knowledge but weak follow-through can create confusion across an entire project team.

Set the New Hire Up for Success

Hiring the right person is only part of the process. Employers should provide a structured first 30 to 60 days that includes project reviews, site visits, financial reporting expectations, safety procedures, documentation standards, and clear decision-making authority. Introduce the new leader to key subcontractors and internal team members early, then explain how project concerns should be escalated.

Measure performance using practical indicators: schedule reliability, quality corrections, timely reporting, budget awareness, client communication, safety participation, and closeout completeness. These expectations should be discussed from the beginning rather than introduced only after a problem appears.

Build a Stronger Construction Leadership Team

The best Project Manager or Construction Superintendent is not simply the person with the longest résumé. Employers should hire for relevant project experience, technical judgment, communication discipline, field credibility, and the ability to keep people aligned when conditions change. A thoughtful process produces leaders who protect the work, the client relationship, and the team behind the project.
