MEP Coordination Checklists for Employers

Why MEP Coordination Deserves Employer Attention

Mechanical, electrical, and plumbing work occupies some of the most congested space in a building: above ceilings, inside shafts, in equipment rooms, and around structural members. Fire protection piping, ductwork, conduit, cable tray, plumbing lines, controls, and access clearances all compete for the same limited pathways.

When those conflicts are discovered after crews are mobilized, the impact is rarely limited to one trade. A duct reroute may affect sprinkler coverage, electrical conduit pathways, ceiling heights, framing, insulation, and the sequence of several subcontractors. The result can be added labor, material waste, inspection delays, schedule pressure, and disputes over responsibility.

For employers, an MEP coordination checklist is not simply a preconstruction formality. It is a practical control that creates clear decisions before field labor, material deliveries, and installation commitments make changes expensive. With more than two decades of commercial construction experience across fire protection, electrical systems, and general contracting, John Dillon has seen that the strongest coordination process starts with disciplined preparation—not last-minute problem solving in the field.

What an MEP Coordination Checklist Should Accomplish

A useful checklist does more than ask whether drawings have been reviewed. It identifies the information each trade needs, establishes the order in which conflicts will be resolved, and documents who owns each decision. Employers should expect the checklist to produce coordinated, buildable work—not just meeting notes.

Before construction starts, the coordination process should confirm:

  • Current architectural, structural, civil, and MEP drawings are available to all involved parties.
  • Equipment locations, dimensions, weights, utility requirements, and manufacturer installation clearances have been verified.
  • Ceiling heights, soffits, shafts, chases, roof penetrations, and equipment-room layouts have been reviewed as shared spaces.
  • Trade priorities and installation sequence have been established for congested areas.
  • Required access for valves, dampers, panels, cleanouts, filters, control devices, and fire protection components has been maintained.
  • Unresolved issues are tracked with an assigned owner and a decision deadline.
  • Approved coordination changes are incorporated into the current construction documents and communicated to field supervisors.

Start With Complete, Current Information

Coordination cannot succeed when trades are working from mismatched drawing sets. Employers should require a document-control process before the first coordination meeting. Confirm that the architectural reflected ceiling plan, structural framing plans, mechanical plans, electrical plans, plumbing plans, fire protection drawings, equipment submittals, and applicable specifications are current.

Equipment submittals matter because plan symbols do not always show actual dimensions, connection points, service doors, or maintenance space. For example, a rooftop unit may fit on a plan but require duct transitions, electrical disconnect clearance, condensate routing, and service access that conflict with nearby structure or piping. Reviewing the real equipment data early prevents a paper solution from becoming a field problem.

Coordinate the Most Congested Areas First

Not every room carries the same risk. Employers can make coordination meetings more productive by directing the team to review high-conflict areas first. These typically include corridors with low ceilings, mechanical rooms, electrical rooms, kitchens, restroom groups, risers, shafts, loading areas, and spaces with exposed building systems.

Use a Clear Trade-Priority Strategy

Each project should establish a logical hierarchy for routing systems. Structural elements are fixed. Gravity plumbing often needs priority because it depends on slope. Large duct mains may require the next level of consideration, followed by major piping, cable tray, conduit, branch lines, and controls. The exact order can vary by project, but the team needs an agreed method before making changes.

Without a defined approach, a clash meeting can become a series of competing preferences. With one, the superintendent and project manager can guide decisions based on code, constructability, access, system performance, and the overall schedule.

Include Fire Protection and Life-Safety Requirements Early

Fire protection is sometimes brought into coordination too late, especially when sprinkler design is treated as a separate downstream activity. That can create serious conflicts when main piping, branch lines, heads, seismic bracing, and required clearances are added after ductwork or electrical systems are already laid out.

Employers should ensure the checklist includes fire protection drawings, ceiling conditions, obstructions, sprinkler head locations, valve access, fire pump or riser room requirements, and coordination with mechanical diffusers, lights, and ceiling grids. John Dillon’s background in fire protection and electrical work reinforces an important point: life-safety systems must be coordinated as core building systems, not accommodated after the fact.

Verify Constructability, Not Just Drawing Compatibility

A coordinated layout can still fail if it cannot be installed safely and efficiently. During review, ask practical field questions:

  • Can installers physically access the work area with tools, lifts, and materials?
  • Is there enough room for hangers, supports, seismic bracing, insulation, and fittings?
  • Can equipment be set before surrounding work blocks the path?
  • Will one trade’s installation prevent another trade from completing inspections or testing?
  • Are service panels, shutoff valves, cleanouts, dampers, and equipment filters reachable after turnover?
  • Does the proposed routing preserve required code clearances and manufacturer requirements?

For example, moving conduit to avoid a duct may appear simple on a coordination drawing. In the field, that new route may interfere with access to a sprinkler control valve or force excessive bends that complicate installation. Constructability review catches these second-order problems before they become change requests.

Assign Ownership and Close Open Items

Employers should not allow coordination issues to remain as vague notes such as “review in field.” Every conflict should be entered into a coordination log with the location, affected systems, proposed resolution, responsible party, required decision date, and final status. The project manager should confirm whether the resolution changes cost, schedule, scope, or permitting requirements.

Once a decision is approved, distribute the updated information to the people who need it: design professionals, subcontractor project managers, foremen, procurement teams, and field supervision. A resolved clash that never reaches the crew is still a field clash waiting to happen.

Make Coordination a Preconstruction Standard

MEP coordination is most effective when employers treat it as a required preconstruction deliverable rather than an optional meeting. Set expectations in subcontract scopes, establish milestone dates, require accountable attendance, and avoid releasing major installation work in congested areas until coordination is complete.

For commercial and residential projects across the greater Nashville area, the goal is straightforward: give experienced field leaders enough reliable information to build the work once, in the right sequence, with fewer surprises. A thorough MEP coordination checklist protects the budget and schedule while supporting safer, cleaner installation.
