Managing Multi-Site Commercial Construction Projects

John Dillon

At any given point running IKON Electric, it wasn't unusual to have crews working several commercial sites in the same week — a retail tenant build-out, a dealership service bay, and a smaller residential or commercial repair call, all at once. Multi-site project management is a different discipline than running one job well; it's about building systems that don't depend on you being everywhere.

Scheduling has to be realistic, not optimistic

The biggest failure point in multi-site work is a schedule built on best-case assumptions — every delivery on time, every inspection passing first try, no weather delays. Real schedules need buffer built in, and they need to be revisited constantly as jobs actually progress, not set once and forgotten.

Know which sites need you in person

Not every site needs the owner walking it daily, but some do — the first day on a new commercial client, any point where scope is being defined on the fly, or when a general contractor needs a decision made immediately to keep their own schedule intact. Part of managing multiple sites well is being honest about which ones actually need your physical presence versus a phone call.

Subcontractor and crew coordination

Materials, manpower, and subcontractors all have to be coordinated across sites without conflicts — the same crew or the same delivery truck can't be in two places. This is where clear communication with every party (property owners, GCs, and your own crews) makes the difference between a job that runs on schedule and one that quietly slips.

Consistency across sites builds trust

Clients with multiple locations — automotive dealership groups, retail chains — notice when the quality and communication on site three matches site one. That consistency is what turns a single project into a repeat relationship, which is how a lot of the strongest commercial relationships I built over the years actually started.
