Why Fire Protection and Electrical Code Compliance Go Hand in Hand

John Dillon

Most people think of fire protection and electrical work as two separate trades — one deals with sprinklers and alarms, the other with panels and wiring. In practice, on almost every commercial project I've worked, the two are tangled together more than property owners expect.

A big share of fire code violations I've seen on commercial properties trace back to electrical issues: overloaded panels, improper junction box installations, missing arc-fault protection, or wiring run too close to fire-rated penetrations without proper firestopping. The National Electrical Code (NEC) and the fire codes that govern life-safety systems aren't written in isolation — they're meant to work together, and inspectors check both.

Where the overlap actually shows up

  • Firestopping around electrical penetrations. Any time a conduit, cable tray, or wire run passes through a fire-rated wall or floor, that penetration needs to be properly sealed. It's an easy thing to miss during a rushed build-out, and it's one of the first things a fire marshal checks.
  • Emergency and egress lighting circuits. These have to be wired correctly, tested on a schedule, and kept on dedicated, properly protected circuits — not an afterthought tacked onto a general lighting panel.
  • Fire alarm and suppression system power. Fire protection systems need reliable, code-compliant electrical supply and backup power. When electrical and fire protection contractors aren't coordinating, this is where things get missed.
  • Panel labeling and access. Fire inspectors and electrical inspectors both care about clear panel labeling and unobstructed access — for very different reasons, but the fix is the same.

What this means for property owners and GCs

If you're managing a commercial build-out or renovation, the practical takeaway is simple: don't treat your fire protection contractor and your electrical contractor as two unrelated line items. The projects that go smoothly are the ones where those trades are coordinating early, not discovering conflicts during final inspection.

Having spent years on the fire and rescue side before moving into electrical contracting, this is the lens I bring to every commercial job — code compliance isn't a box to check at the end, it's part of how the work gets planned from day one.
